### [Fingerprints are the new cookies](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50048642)

- **HN engagement:** 585 points, 287 comments
- **Primary topic:** Browser fingerprinting as a tracking mechanism replacing third-party cookies
- **Evidence basis:** source-and-discussion
- **External page:** success
- **Source:** https://royfish.dev/fingerprints-are-the-new-cookies

Fingerprinting shifts the tracking surface from storage (clearable cookies) to hardware and browser API behavior that users cannot easily reset, raising questions about which browser APIs should require consent and what effective privacy-preserving architecture looks like. Developers building analytics, auth, or ad-adjacent systems must understand which signals they are implicitly exposing and what mitigations actually work at the engine level versus the extension level.

**Linked source:** The article demonstrates live browser fingerprinting using OfflineAudioContext, WebGL, Canvas, Intl, and navigator APIs to produce a SHA-256 hash that persists across cookie clears and VPN use, then explains information entropy as the mechanism that makes combined hardware signals uniquely identifying. It frames Google's Privacy Sandbox detour and eventual cookie retention as deliberate delay while fingerprinting-based ad products advanced, and recommends only engine-level browser protections (Brave, Tor, Mullvad) as effective defenses.

**HN discussion:** Commenters debated whether fingerprinting is practically stable enough for commercial ad targeting, with one commenter arguing IP address still dominates real-world fingerprinting services and that non-IP signals degrade within days. Others focused on why high-entropy browser APIs such as WebGL and audio processing are not gated behind explicit user consent, and one commenter pushed back on the article's framing by noting EU law requires consent for any tracking method, not just cookies.

Marketplace partners and API consumers have no single authoritative place to check whether an Atlassian platform failure caused a customer-reported error, forcing costly manual debugging against siloed status pages. The project also exposes that published uptime figures can remain at 100% even when support privately confirms an active incident, undermining trust in vendor SLA reporting.

**Linked source:** The project claims to consolidate 3,865 incidents from all 19 Atlassian status pages (spanning 2016–2026) into a single queryable JSONL file, built exclusively from public Statuspage v2 endpoints and history archives. The source highlights that declared incidents per page have fallen roughly by half since 2024, that Atlassian's published uptime figures are computed solely from declared outages rather than measured data, and that at least one confirmed 12-hour Confluence Cloud API disruption was never published and left the reported 30-day uptime at 100.0%.
